1. Does the MGF Exist?

A fundamental distinction in probability theory and econometrics is whether a distribution possesses a moment generating function (MGF).

Let

\[ M_X(t) = E[e^{tX}] \]

denote the MGF of a random variable \(X\).

A distribution \(F\) is called heavy-tailed if

\[ M_X(t) = \infty, \qquad \forall t > 0 \]

In contrast, if there exists some \(t>0\) such that

\[ M_X(t) < \infty, \]

the distribution is said to be light-tailed.

This criterion acts as a natural “death line”: once the MGF ceases to exist, classical exponential large-deviation tools break down, and extreme outcomes become fundamentally more likely.

2. The Hierarchy of Heavy Tails

Heavy-tailed distributions themselves form a structured hierarchy.
A commonly used nesting is

\[ \text{Regularly Varying} \subset \text{Subexponential} \subset \text{Long-tailed} \subset \text{Heavy-tailed}. \]

Each step corresponds to progressively weaker assumptions about how slowly the tail decays.

2.1 Long-tailed distributions

A distribution belongs to the long-tailed class if

\[ \frac{\bar F(x+y)}{\bar F(x)} \to 1 \quad \text{as } x \to \infty \]

for every fixed \(y\), where

\[ \bar F(x) = P(X>x) \]

is the survival function.

Intuitively, adding a fixed constant \(y\) barely changes the tail probability when \(x\) is large.

2.2 Subexponential distributions and the “Single Big Jump”

A central subclass of heavy tails is the subexponential class, defined by

\[ \overline{F^{*2}}(x) \sim 2\bar F(x), \quad x \to \infty. \]

Equivalently,

\[ P(X_1 + X_2 > x) \sim 2P(X > x) \]

for independent copies \(X_1, X_2\).

This property leads to the Single Big Jump Principle:

When a sum of subexponential variables becomes extremely large, it is typically caused by one extreme observation, rather than by the accumulation of many moderate values.

2.3 Regular variation (power-law tails)

The most widely studied heavy tails are regularly varying tails, characterized by

\[ \frac{\bar F(tx)}{\bar F(x)} \to t^{-\alpha}, \quad t>0. \]

Equivalently,

\[ \bar F(x) = x^{-\alpha} L(x), \]

where \(L(x)\) is a slowly varying function.

Regular variation provides a tractable mathematical structure and plays a central role in extreme value theory and financial tail modeling.

3. Light Tails: The “Conspiracy of Smalls”

Light-tailed distributions behave very differently from heavy-tailed ones.

For light tails, the probability of producing one extremely large observation is exponentially small. As a result, extreme sums typically arise through the combined effect of multiple moderately large values, rather than a single outlier.

For example, suppose

\[ f(x) \propto e^{-x^2}. \]

To produce a sum of \(10\):

  • Single jump: \((10 + 0)\) gives probability \(\propto e^{-100}\)
  • Two moderate values: \((5 + 5)\) gives probability \(\propto e^{-50}\)

Since \(e^{-50} \gg e^{-100}\), the second scenario dominates.

This phenomenon explains why Gaussian-type distributions do not satisfy the subexponential property.

4. Summary of Tail Classes

Class Mathematical Criterion Typical Examples
Light-tailed \(M_X(t)<\infty\) for some \(t>0\) Normal, Exponential
Heavy-tailed \(M_X(t)=\infty\) for all \(t>0\) Lognormal, Pareto
Long-tailed \(\bar F(x+y)/\bar F(x)\to1\) Pareto, Lognormal
Subexponential \(\overline{F^{*2}}(x)/\bar F(x)\to2\) Pareto, Lognormal
Regularly varying \(\bar F(tx)/\bar F(x)\to t^{-\alpha}\) Pareto, Student-t

5. Conclusion

Tail behavior determines how extreme events arise.

  • In light-tailed environments, extreme outcomes result from a conspiracy of many moderate deviations.
  • In subexponential environments, extremes are dominated by a single big jump.

Understanding which regime a model belongs to is crucial for applications ranging from risk management to econometric modeling of financial volatility.
